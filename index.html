Space Shock · Research overview

When Rockets Fail, Who Pays the Price?

An empirical investigation of financial contagion and competitive effects following rocket launch failures.

When an orbital rocket fails, what happens to the stock of the company that built it, and to the stocks of its listed competitors? Investors could treat the failure as bad news for the whole industry (contagion) or as a chance for rivals to win customers (competitive gain). This study measures which, using 138 orbital launch failures from 2000 to 2026.

Key findings

Rocket makers

−8.8% to −16.3%

Two-day abnormal return in each of the 4 listed-maker cases (2 firms). Case studies, not an average.

Listed competitors

+0.28%

Average two-day abnormal return. 95% interval −1.62% to +2.18%.

Statistical test

p = 0.40

Prespecified standardized test. No conclusive evidence of either contagion or competitive gains.

Sample

22 independent events

42 reactions of 3 listed competitor firms to 24 failures.

What the evidence can and cannot show

  • Makers lose. In every listed case the maker's shares fell well beyond the market over two days. With 4 cases this describes those cases, not every launch company.
  • Competitors: imprecise, not zero. The evidence does not establish a statistically reliable competitive or contagion effect; it does not show that there is no effect either. The interval runs from −1.62% to +2.18%.
  • Low power. Average effects below about 2.7% either way would usually be missed.
  • Small, concentrated sample. 3 listed competitors; Avio SpA is in all 22 independent events.
  • Weighting matters. Weighting every reaction equally gives +0.28%; weighting every firm equally gives −1.16% (a sensitivity analysis, not the prespecified estimate).
  • Event timing. 88% of competitor reactions are dated from GCAT launch times not separately checked against a company statement, used only where the trading day would not change under a small timing error.
