When a rocket blows up, who loses money, and does anyone win?

How stock markets react to orbital launch failures, 2000 to 2026.

In short

When an orbital rocket fails, does the stock market punish only the company that built it, or the whole launch industry? Using 138 orbital launch failures from 2000 to 2026, this study measures abnormal stock returns over the two trading days after each failure became public. In the 4 cases where the rocket's maker was listed, its shares fell by between 8.8% and 16.3% after allowing for the market. Listed competitors moved by +0.28% on average (95% interval −1.62% to +2.18%; p = 0.40), which is not statistically detectable with 22 independent events. The evidence fits a simple reading: investors treat a failure as bad news for the firm that flew the rocket, not as news about the industry. Every analysis choice was fixed in a written plan before any stock reaction was measured.

Why this question

Orbital launch is a small industry with a handful of listed firms, and every launch is a public, all-or-nothing test. A failure destroys hardware and a customer's satellite, so the rocket's maker should lose value. The open question is the competitors. If investors read a failure as a reminder that spaceflight is risky for everyone, rival launch stocks fall too: contagion. If they expect customers to switch to a rocket that works, rivals rise: competitive gain. The two stories point in opposite directions, so only data can say which one investors act on.

The same question appears wherever one firm's bad news may spread to similar firms: bank failures, product recalls, sovereign debt crises. Launch failures are useful because they are sudden, public and easy to date.

What the data say

For the rocket's maker, the answer is the same in every case: shares fell over the two days after the failure became public, after allowing for the market. With 4 cases, these are case studies, not an industry average.

For competitors, the average reaction is not statistically detectable: +0.28% over two days, from 24 failures, 3 companies and 22 independent events. "Not detectable" is not the same as "no effect", but the interval tells us average reactions much larger than its ends, in either direction, are unlikely. 12 of 12 prespecified robustness checks reach the same conclusion. An exploratory check added after the results, which leaves out two days when competitors announced large contracts, gives −0.70% (95% interval −1.80% to +0.39%); it is labelled as such everywhere.

Read economically, a launch failure looks like a firm-specific shock. Investors do not seem to rewrite their view of the industry, and there is no sign that competitors are expected to win business quickly.

How it was done

  • Sample. Orbital launch failures since 2000 from GCAT (J. McDowell), linked by hand to listed makers and to listed competitors of a similar size selling to the same customers.
  • Measure. Abnormal return: the stock's actual return minus what a market model, fitted on about a year of earlier data, predicts from its home market index.
  • Test. A standardized event-study test (Boehmer, Musumeci and Poulsen) adjusted for failures that hit several competitors at once (Kolari and Pynnönen), with cluster-robust intervals.
  • Checks. Other test statistics, a wider window, timing and sample changes, leaving out one firm at a time, and a placebo using random ordinary days.
  • Rules. The plan was frozen and committed before results; anything added later is labelled exploratory. Only derived statistics are published, never stock prices.
