When a rocket blows up, who loses money, and does anyone win?

How stock markets react to orbital launch failures, 2000 to 2026.

In short

When an orbital rocket fails, does the stock market punish only the company that built it, or its listed competitors too? Using 138 orbital launch failures from 2000 to 2026, this study measures abnormal stock returns over the two trading days after each failure became public, with a market model and a prespecified event-study test. In the 4 cases where the rocket's maker was listed, its shares fell by between 8.8% and 16.3% after allowing for the market. Across 24 failures with listed competitors (42 stock reactions, 3 firms, 22 independent events), the average reaction was +0.28% (95% interval −1.62% to +2.18%, p = 0.76; the prespecified standardized test gives p = 0.40). The data therefore do not establish either contagion or competitive gains among these firms. They also do not show that the effect is small: average reactions of about 2.7% in either direction could not have been reliably detected. The evidence comes from 3 listed firms, with Avio SpA in every independent event, and the sign of the average changes with how events and firms are weighted.

1. The research question

Orbital launch is a small industry with a handful of listed firms, and every launch is a public, all-or-nothing test. A failure destroys hardware and often a customer's satellite, so the rocket's maker should lose value. The open question is the competitors. If investors read a failure as a reminder that spaceflight is risky for everyone, rival launch stocks fall too: contagion. If they expect customers to move to a rocket that works, rivals rise: competitive gain. The two stories point in opposite directions, and both could operate at once.

The same question appears wherever one firm's bad news may spread to similar firms: bank failures, product recalls, sovereign debt crises. Launch failures are useful because they are sudden, public and easy to date.

2. What the data include

The numbers below count different things, so they cannot be read as one shrinking funnel:

  • Launch failures (138): every orbital launch from 2000 to 2026 that GCAT scores below 0.75.
  • Listed-maker cases (4): failures whose rocket was built by a company listed on a stock exchange at the time.
  • Rival-eligible failures (24): failures with at least one listed direct competitor (similar rocket size, same customers, launch a major business).
  • Rival reactions (42): one per listed rival per failure; one failure can produce several.
  • Independent events (22): failures that share a firm within a few trading days are grouped, because their reactions are not independent.
  • Distinct rival firms (3): Avio SpA, Firefly Aerospace, Rocket Lab.

Method in brief. For each stock, a market model fitted on about a year of earlier data predicts the return given its home market index; the abnormal return is the actual return minus that prediction, summed over the two trading days after the failure became public. Only derived statistics are published, never stock prices. The rules were committed to the public repository before results (commit record in the working paper, Appendix G); they were not filed with an independent registry.

3. What happened to the rocket's maker?

In every listed case the maker's shares fell over the two days after the failure became public, after allowing for the market. Two-day abnormal returns ranged from −8.8% to −16.3%. With 4 cases from 2 firms, these are case studies, not a general launch-provider effect, and abnormal returns are not exact monetary losses caused by the failure.

4. What happened to its competitors?

The average two-day reaction was +0.28% (95% interval −1.62% to +2.18%, p = 0.76), which is not statistically detectable. The prespecified standardized test, which gives less weight to volatile stocks and so asks a slightly different question, also finds nothing detectable (p = 0.40). Each p-value is reported next to the quantity it tests.

5. How reliable is the result?

The sample is small and concentrated: 3 firms, and Avio SpA is in every one of the 22 independent events, so the events are not independent draws from the whole industry.

With this sample, an average effect smaller than about 2.7% in either direction would usually go undetected (80% power at the 5% level); the chance of detecting a true 1% effect is about 18%. So "not detectable" here does not mean "no effect": the interval contains economically meaningful losses and gains.

The result is consistent with several explanations: contagion and competitive gain cancelling out, customers being unable to switch rockets quickly, or investors treating a failure mainly as news about the failed firm. The data cannot tell these apart.

6. What changes under robustness checks?

Prespecified checks. None of the 12 checks fixed in advance detects a significant average at the 5% level, but the estimates range from −0.15% to +1.84%: the lack of a detectable effect is stable, its direction and size are not. The real result sits at the 61st percentile of averages on random ordinary days.

Weighting (sensitivity, added after the results). +0.28% with every reaction equal (the prespecified estimate), +0.21% with every event equal, +0.87% weighting calm stocks more, −1.16% with every firm equal. No weighting is statistically preferred.

Exploratory. An exploratory check added after the results leaves out the 2 days when competitors announced large contracts: the average moves to −0.70% (95% interval −1.80% to +0.39%), while the median moves only from +0.28% to +0.09%, so the average is sensitive to a few large moves. It is exploratory and does not replace the main result.

7. What does the evidence establish?

  • Shown: large two-day abnormal losses for the listed maker in each observed case; no detectable average reaction among the 3 listed competitors studied.
  • Not shown: that competitors systematically lose (contagion) or gain (competition); that the effect is negligible; that customers switch providers; that industry-wide reactions are absent; that the pattern holds for private, state-owned or diversified aerospace firms.
