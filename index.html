When a rocket fails, who loses money?

An event study of 138 orbital launch failures, 2000 to 2026: the stock of the company that built the rocket, and the stocks of its listed competitors.

T−4 · The question

Contagion or a gain for rivals?

A failed launch destroys a rocket and often a customer's satellite, so the builder should lose value. The open question is its listed competitors. If investors read a failure as a sign that spaceflight is risky for everyone, rival stocks fall: contagion. If they expect customers to move to a rocket that works, rival stocks rise: competitive gain.

The same question comes up when a bank fails or a product is recalled. Launch failures are useful because they are sudden, public and easy to date.

T−3 · What the data show

Two findings

01 · The rocket's maker

−8.8% to −16.3%

In all 4 listed cases, the maker's shares fell 8.8% to 16.3% more than the market in the two trading days after the failure.

02 · Its listed competitors

+0.28%

Listed competitors moved +0.28% on average (95% interval −1.62% to +2.18%, p = 0.76). That is not statistically detectable. 22 independent events, 3 firms.

The prespecified standardized test gives p = 0.40. On random ordinary days the real average sits at the 61st percentile.

T−2 · How it works

Three steps

  1. 1

    Find every failure

    138 orbital launch failures from GCAT, each dated to the first trading day after the news became public.

  2. 2

    Predict a normal day

    A market model, fitted on about a year of earlier returns, predicts each stock's return from its home index.

  3. 3

    Measure the gap

    The abnormal return is actual minus predicted, added up over the two trading days after the failure.

The sample in detail
  • Listed-maker cases (4): failures whose rocket was built by a listed company.
  • Rival-eligible failures (24): failures with at least one listed direct competitor.
  • Rival reactions (42): one per listed rival per failure.
  • Independent events (22): failures that share a firm within a few trading days count as one event.
  • Rival firms (3): Avio SpA, Firefly Aerospace, Rocket Lab.

The rules were committed to the public repository before the results (record in the working paper, Appendix G); they were not filed with an independent registry. Only derived statistics are published.

T−1 · What it means

What the evidence can and cannot say

Shown

Large two-day losses for the maker in every listed case. No detectable average reaction among the 3 listed competitors.

Not shown

That rivals lose or gain on average; that the effect is zero; that customers switch rockets; that the pattern holds for private or state-owned firms.

Why so uncertain

Only 3 listed rivals, and Avio SpA is in all 22 events. Effects below about 2.7% either way would usually be missed.

Reliability and robustness

With this sample, an average effect smaller than about 2.7% either way would usually go undetected (80% power at the 5% level); the chance of detecting a true 1% effect is about 18%. "Not detectable" here does not mean "no effect".

Prespecified checks. None of the 12 checks fixed in advance detects a significant average at the 5% level; the estimates range from −0.15% to +1.84%, so the lack of an effect is stable but its direction and size are not.

Weighting (added after the results). +0.28% with every reaction equal (prespecified), +0.21% with every event equal, +0.87% weighting calm stocks more, −1.16% with every firm equal.

Exploratory. An exploratory check added after the results leaves out the 2 days when competitors announced large contracts: the average moves to −0.70% (95% interval −1.80% to +0.39%), while the median moves only from +0.28% to +0.09%, so the average is sensitive to a few large moves. It is exploratory and does not replace the main result.

Possible explanations the data cannot separate: contagion and competitive gain cancelling out, customers unable to switch quickly, or investors treating a failure as news about the failed firm only.
