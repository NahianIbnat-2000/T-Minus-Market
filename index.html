Space finance · Event study · Working paper

When rockets fail, who pays the price?

What happens to stock prices when an orbital rocket fails: for the company that built it, and for its listed competitors. 138 failures, 2000 to 2026.

Explore the interactive reportRead the working paperDownload PDF

Nahian Ibnat · Independent working paper, not peer reviewed · Results Nov 2026

01Headline findings

The rocket's maker

−8.8% to −16.3%

Two-day abnormal return (cumulative, day 0 to +1) in each of the 4 listed-maker cases, from 2 firms.

Its listed competitors

+0.28%

Average two-day abnormal return, 95% interval −1.62% to +2.18%. 42 reactions of 3 listed competitor firms to 24 failures, grouped into 22 independent events.

Prespecified test

Standardized (BMP) test with a correction for events on nearby days: p = 0.40. It gives volatile stocks less weight.

Mean test

Cluster-robust test of the plain average shown above: p = 0.76. Same data, a different question, so the two p-values are not interchangeable.

02In plain language

A failed launch is expensive for the company that built the rocket: in every listed case its shares fell sharply, more than the market, over the next two trading days. For its competitors the picture is unclear. Their average reaction is close to zero, but the interval runs from −1.62% to +2.18%, so losses or gains of up to about 2.7% cannot be ruled out. Not finding an effect is not the same as finding no effect.

03Contagion or competition?

When one company's rocket fails, investors can read the news about its competitors in two opposite ways.

↘

Contagion

The failure is a reminder that spaceflight is risky for everyone: insurance, delays, customer doubts. Rival stocks fall.

or

↗

Competitive gain

Customers may move their satellites to a rocket that works. Rival stocks rise.

Both can happen at once and partly cancel out, so an average near zero is consistent with either story, with both, or with neither.

06Limitations and uncertainty

Low statistical power

Average effects smaller than about 2.7% in either direction would usually be missed (80% power, 5% level). The chance of detecting a true 1% effect is about 18%.

Few firms

Only 3 listed competitors, and Avio SpA appears in all 22 independent events. Results may not hold for private or state-owned launch firms.

Weighting changes the sign

Average two-day reaction under different weightings
Every reaction equal (prespecified)+0.28%
Every event equal+0.21%
Calmer stocks weigh more+0.87%
Every firm equal−1.16%
Median reaction+0.28%

Sensitivity added after the results. No weighting is statistically preferred.

Confounding news Exploratory

Dropping the 2 reactions on days when competitors announced large contracts moves the average to −0.70% (95% interval −1.80% to +0.39%); the median moves only from +0.28% to +0.09%. Chosen after the results, so it does not replace the main test.

Robustness

None of the 12 prespecified checks finds a significant average at the 5% level; their estimates range from −0.15% to +1.84%. On random ordinary days the real average sits at the 61st percentile.

What an abnormal return is not

It is the return beyond what the market model predicts, not an exact money loss caused by the failure. The rules were committed to the public repository before the results, not filed with an independent registry.
