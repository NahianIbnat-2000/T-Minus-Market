Abstract

This paper asks how equity markets react when an orbital launch fails, both for the company that built the rocket and for its publicly listed competitors. Using 138 orbital launch failures from 2000 to 2026 (GCAT) and a market-model event study with a prespecified test, it finds that in the 4 failures with a listed maker (2 firms) the maker's two-day cumulative abnormal return was between −8.8% and −16.3%, each case with a 95% interval excluding zero. Across 24 failures with listed competitors (42 stock reactions, 3 firms, 22 independent events), the equal-weighted average two-day cumulative abnormal return is +0.28% (95% cluster-robust interval −1.62% to +2.18%; cluster-robust p = 0.76); the prespecified standardized test (Boehmer-Musumeci-Poulsen with a Kolari-Pynnönen adjustment) gives p = 0.40. The evidence therefore establishes neither systematic contagion nor systematic competitive gains among these firms, and it does not show the effect is negligible: average effects of about 2.7% in either direction would usually go undetected. The sign of the estimate changes with how events and firms are weighted, and every independent event involves Avio SpA. The study is a small-sample study of listed firms, not an estimate for the launch industry as a whole.

Keywords: event study; industry spillovers; contagion; competitive effects; launch failures

1. Introduction

When a rocket fails, the company that built it loses the rocket, often a customer's satellite, and usually several months of flights while engineers find the cause. In the United States, for example, a licensed launch operator must investigate the root cause of any mishap, report the results to the regulator, and put in place measures that stop it from happening again (Federal Aviation Administration, 2021). How the share prices of the maker's competitors move around a failure is much less clear, and it is the main question of this paper.

Economists describe two opposite ways in which bad news at one firm can move the share prices of other firms in the same industry (Lang & Stulz, 1992). The first is contagion: investors read the failure as news about the whole industry, for example that rockets are riskier than they thought or that rules will become stricter, and they lower their estimate of the value of every firm in it. The second is the competitive effect: investors expect customers to move their satellites to providers whose rockets still work, so the competitors gain. The two channels push share prices in opposite directions, so theory alone cannot say which one is larger. Only data can.

The question matters more now than it did a decade ago, because launch companies have become a larger part of public stock markets. In June 2026 SpaceX began trading on the Nasdaq after raising about $75 billion, the largest initial public offering on record (“SpaceX Makes Its Nasdaq Debut,” 2026). When more launch firms are listed, more investors hold shares whose value depends on how the market prices launch risk, both the risk of a firm's own rockets and the risk of its competitors' rockets. Whether a failure at one provider moves the shares of the others matters for how investors diversify, for how they value new listings, and for how firms and regulators think about the industry's exposure to a single accident.

Earlier work has answered the same question for other industries. Studies of bankruptcies (Lang & Stulz, 1992), bank failures (Aharony & Swary, 1983), product recalls (Jarrell & Peltzman, 1985) and air crashes (Bosch et al., 1998; Chance & Ferris, 1987) show that bad news at one firm often moves the share prices of its competitors, and that the direction depends on the industry. For spaceflight, the evidence comes mainly from a single event, the loss of the space shuttle Challenger in 1986 (Blose et al., 1996; Maloney & Mulherin, 2003), and from studies of satellite operators, who are the launch firms' customers rather than their competitors (Abramov & Kiang, 2015). To the author's knowledge, no study has measured how listed launch providers react to the failures of their competitors across many events.

This paper fills that gap with an event study of orbital launch failures from 2000 to 2026. An event study measures how much a share price moves on and just after a specific piece of news, beyond what the general movement of the market on those days would predict (MacKinlay, 1997). Launch failures suit the method well. They happen at a known time, they are usually broadcast live, and they are not caused by the decisions of the competitors whose shares are studied. The paper makes three contributions. First, it builds a hand-checked list of which listed firms were real competitors of the failed rocket on the day of each failure, using the GCAT launch catalogue (McDowell, n.d.). Second, it reports the reaction of the failed rocket's maker as a set of case studies and tests one confirmatory hypothesis for competitors, called H2: the average two-day abnormal return of listed competitors is zero. All rules for this test were fixed in the project's public repository before any result was computed (Appendix G). Third, it reports the uncertainty in full: how small an effect the sample could detect, how the estimate changes when events and firms are weighted differently, and which reactions coincide with other company news.

The answer is limited by the sample. Only 3 listed launch firms qualify as direct competitors at the time of a failure, so the study observes 22 independent competitor events, every one of which involves Avio SpA. The average reaction of competitors is not distinguishable from zero, but the interval around it is wide enough to contain losses and gains that would matter to investors. The contribution is therefore a carefully documented and reproducible estimate with its uncertainty stated in full, not a general claim about the launch industry.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the launch industry and section 3 reviews earlier studies. Section 4 and section 5 describe the data and the method. Section 6, section 7 and section 8 report the main results, the robustness checks and the role of other company news. Section 9 discusses the findings, section 10 sets out the limitations and section 11 concludes.

2. Institutional background

An orbital launch places a satellite or another payload into orbit around the Earth. A small number of providers sell these launches, and many of them are government agencies, state-owned companies or privately held firms. As a result, only a few launch providers have shares that trade on a stock exchange, and these are the only firms whose market value can be observed every day. Rockets and satellites need long preparation before a launch, so a provider cannot easily turn spare rockets into launches in the near term, and satellite operators must choose from a limited number of providers and rockets (Dillow, 2015). This matters for the competitive effect: even if customers want to leave a provider after a failure, the competitors can take on only a limited amount of new business in the short run.

Launching is risky, and new rockets are riskier than mature ones. A study of launch records from 1980 to 2017 by NASA engineers found that new launch vehicles have had much higher failure probabilities in their early flights than engineering risk models predicted (Cross & Vesely, 2018). After a failure, the rocket normally stops flying while the cause is found and fixed; in the United States this investigation is a legal requirement for licensed launches (Federal Aviation Administration, 2021). For the maker, a failure therefore means the loss of the rocket, possible penalties or free replacement flights for the customer, the delay of its next launches, and damage to its reputation for reliability.

Few launch companies have been publicly traded with launch as a major part of their business. Large aerospace groups that build rockets also make aircraft, defence systems or satellites, so a launch failure is a small part of their value and their share price says little about their launch business. The study therefore counts a firm as a direct competitor only when launch is its only business (a pure-play firm) or a major one (Appendix B). The listed launch-focused firms in the sample became public mostly in the last decade, so most of the competitor evidence comes from recent years. SpaceX listed on the Nasdaq in June 2026 (“SpaceX Makes Its Nasdaq Debut,” 2026) and had too little trading history to enter the sample, because the model in section 5 needs at least 100 days of returns before an event.

Most orbital launches are shown live online, so a failure usually becomes public at the moment it happens. In some cases a launch first appears to succeed, and the company confirms only later that the satellite did not reach the right orbit. In those cases the event date is set by the first public statement from the company, and the paper records how confident it is about that time (section 4).

3. Related literature

3.1 Event studies

The event study is the standard tool for measuring how a piece of news changes the value of a firm. The method goes back to Fama et al. (1969), who studied how share prices adjust to stock splits. Its logic is simple. If investors use public information quickly, the effect of news on a firm's value shows up in its share price within a short time. The researcher predicts what the firm's return would have been without the news, using the firm's normal relation to the market, and calls the difference between the actual and the predicted return the abnormal return (MacKinlay, 1997). Brown and Warner (1985) show that this approach works well with daily returns and simple models.

Two statistical problems matter for this paper. First, a dramatic event can make returns more volatile than usual, and ordinary tests then find an effect too often even when there is none. Boehmer et al. (1991) propose a standardized test that allows for this extra volatility. Second, when one event hits several firms on the same day, their abnormal returns are correlated, which also makes ordinary tests too confident. Kolari and Pynnönen (2010) measure the size of this bias and propose the correction used here. Corrado (1989) offers a test based on ranks rather than averages, which is less sensitive to a few very large returns. Finally, when observations fall into a small number of groups, standard errors that allow for correlation within groups can still be unreliable, and Cameron et al. (2008) and Webb (2023) propose bootstrap methods for this case.

3.2 Contagion and competitive effects

A large literature asks how news at one firm moves its competitors. Lang and Stulz (1992) separate the two channels described in the introduction. They find that bankruptcy announcements lower the value of a portfolio of competitors by about 1% on average, so contagion dominates on average, but that competitors gain in highly concentrated industries with low debt, where the bankrupt firm's customers can move to its rivals. Aharony and Swary (1983) study the failures of large US banks and find that the effect on other banks depends on whether the cause of the failure is specific to the failed bank or could be shared by others. Jarrell and Peltzman (1985) show that product recalls in the drug and car industries cost the recalling firm more than the direct cost of the recall, and that part of the loss spreads to its competitors.

3.3 Disasters and investor sentiment

Accidents are a closer comparison to launch failures, because they are sudden, public and physical. Chance and Ferris (1987) study the share prices of airlines after fatal crashes. Bosch et al. (1998) ask whether rival airlines lose because travellers become afraid of flying in general, or gain because travellers switch to other carriers. Kaplanski and Levy (2010) show that aviation disasters are followed by losses across the whole stock market that are far larger than the economic damage of the crash and that reverse within a few days. This points to investor sentiment, meaning a change in mood rather than in information about firm values, as a possible driver of short-run reactions to disasters.

3.4 Spaceflight and launch markets

For spaceflight, most of the evidence comes from one event, the loss of the space shuttle Challenger in January 1986. Blose et al. (1996) study how the shares of the shuttle's contractors reacted and how quickly the market absorbed the news. Maloney and Mulherin (2003) show that the contractors' shares fell together on the day of the disaster, and that within the same day the market singled out Morton Thiokol, the firm whose booster seal was later found to have failed, months before the official inquiry reached that conclusion. Abramov and Kiang (2015) study launch delays and failures from the point of view of satellite operators, the launch firms' customers. Do et al. (2023) find that US satellite launch days draw investors' attention away from firm-specific news, so that share prices move more closely with the market on those days.

3.5 What this paper adds

This paper differs from these studies in three ways. It studies the failed provider's listed competitors, not its contractors or its customers. It uses many failures rather than one, so that an average reaction can be estimated, even though the number of listed competitors is small. And it applies the corrections for extra volatility and same-day correlation discussed above, together with a full report of the study's statistical power, so that a result of "no detectable effect" can be read correctly: as a statement about what the sample can and cannot detect, not as proof that there is no effect.

4. Data and sample construction

4.1 Launch records

The list of launches comes from GCAT, the General Catalog of Artificial Space Objects maintained by the astronomer Jonathan McDowell (McDowell, n.d.). GCAT records orbital launch attempts with their date, time, rocket, launch site and outcome. Each launch has a success score between 0 and 1, where lower scores mean worse outcomes. This paper treats a launch as a failure when its score is below 0.75. Explosions on the launch pad before lift-off are recorded separately and are not part of the main sample. The sample covers 2000 to 2026 and contains 138 failures.

4.2 Firms and competitors

Each failure is linked by hand to its maker, if the maker was a listed company on that date, and to any listed competitors. The link is made point in time, which means that it uses only what was true on the day of the failure: a firm that listed later, or that bought a launch business later, is not counted. A firm is a Tier 1 competitor when four conditions hold on the date of the failure. Its shares are listed on a stock exchange; launch is its only business or a major one; it sells a rocket of the same size class to the same customer market as the failed rocket; and it has flown that rocket in the previous 730 days, which is about two years. A small number of further competitors (Tier 2) are added case by case, each with a documented source. Every row of the firm map used in the test is checked against company filings or exchange notices (Appendix B).

4.3 Share prices

Daily share prices, adjusted for dividends and stock splits, come from Yahoo Finance (Yahoo Finance, n.d.). A daily return is the percentage change in this adjusted price from one trading day to the next. Each firm's benchmark is the main stock index of its home market, so that the model compares a firm with the market in which it trades. Prices from before a firm's real listing, for example the price of a shell company that it merged with in order to list, are dropped. Raw prices are used only on the author's computer and are not redistributed; the paper and the website publish only derived statistics.

4.4 Event dates and independent events

Day 0, written T0, is the first trading session on the stock's own exchange that starts at or after the moment the failure became public. If a rocket fails after the exchange has closed, day 0 is the next trading day. Other days are counted in trading days around day 0: T−1 is the session before it and T+1 the session after it. When two failures less than 5 trading days apart affect the same firm, their windows overlap and the reactions cannot be separated, so they are grouped into one cluster, which this paper calls an independent event. Figure 1 shows how the sample narrows from all launch attempts to the competitor reactions used in the test. The figure matters because the precision of the competitor estimate depends on the number of independent events at the end of this chain, 22, not on the 138 failures at the start: most failures leave the sample because no listed firm was involved.

4.5 Event timing verification

Day 0 depends on when a failure became public relative to the close of the exchange, so the disclosure time of each event was checked. For each event the time is either a sourced company statement, recorded with high or low confidence, or the launch time recorded in GCAT. GCAT times are used only where day 0 would not change if the true disclosure came 10 minutes earlier or up to 180 minutes later; events that fail this check need a sourced time. 88% of competitor reactions (37 of 42) and 4 of 4 maker cases rely on GCAT launch times that were not checked separately against a company statement, and 2 competitor reactions rely on a sourced time with low confidence. Checks R5a and R5b (section 7) test whether these choices matter. Table 1 and Figure 2 give the breakdown. They matter because a wrong day 0 would place the reaction outside the window and pull the measured CAR towards zero; the table shows how much of the evidence rests on times that were not checked against a company statement.

5. Empirical methodology

5.1 Normal and abnormal returns

To measure how share prices move around a failure, the study first needs an estimate of how each stock would normally have moved on those days. This "normal" return comes from the market model, which describes a stock's daily return as a constant plus a fixed share of the market's return (MacKinlay, 1997). The share is called beta (β): a stock with a beta of 1.5 tends to rise 1.5% when the market rises 1%. The model is estimated by ordinary least squares, the standard method that picks the line that best fits past data, on trading days 250 to 30 before day 0. This period, called the estimation window, ends a month before the event so that the event itself cannot affect the estimate. At least 100 daily returns are required. With 30 to 99 returns, the study uses a simpler market-adjusted model, in which the normal return is just the market return (α = 0 and β = 1), and flags the event:

Rit = αi + βi Rmt + εit(1)

In Equation 1, Rit is the return of stock i on day t, Rmt is the return of its market index, and εit is the part of the return that the market does not explain. The abnormal return (AR) on a day is the actual return minus the return the model predicts. The cumulative abnormal return (CAR) over an event window from day τ1 to day τ2 is the sum of the daily abnormal returns:

ARit = Rit − (α̂i + β̂i Rmt),   CARi(τ1, τ2) = Σt=τ1τ2 ARit(2)

A CAR of −10% over two days means that the stock did 10 percentage points worse than the market model predicts, so the firm's market value ended about a tenth lower than its normal relation to the market would suggest. Returns are simple percentage changes, and CARs are sums of simple abnormal returns.

A CAR measures an association: how a stock moved around an event compared with its normal pattern. It can be read as the market's response to the event only if nothing else that matters for the firm happened in the same window. A launch failure is sudden and is not chosen by the competitors, which makes this reading more plausible than for most corporate news, but other company announcements can still fall in the same days (section 8). For this reason the paper describes its estimates as price movements associated with failures, not as proven causal effects.

5.2 How precise is each reaction?

Each CAR contains noise, because even on a quiet day a stock does not move exactly as the model predicts. The variance of each CAR, a measure of how much it would vary by chance, includes both this daily noise and the error from estimating the model itself (Campbell et al., 1997). In Equation 3, σ̂i2 is the variance of the stock's unexplained returns in the estimation window, L is the length of the event window, T the number of estimation days, W the event window and E the estimation window:

Var(CARi) = σ̂i2 [ L + L2/T + (Σt∈W(Rmt − R̄m))2 / Σt∈E(Rmt − R̄m)2 ](3)

5.3 Testing whether competitors react

A statistical test asks whether an average reaction is larger than chance alone would produce. Its result is summarized by a p-value: the probability of seeing an average at least this far from zero if the true average were zero. A p-value below 0.05 is the usual threshold for calling an effect statistically significant. The confirmatory test in this paper is the standardized test of Boehmer et al. (1991), called the BMP test. It first divides each CAR by its own standard deviation, so that every reaction is measured in units of its own noise, and then tests whether the average of these standardized CARs (SCARs) is zero. Because it uses the spread of the standardized CARs across firms, it remains valid when the failure makes returns more volatile than usual:

SCARi = CARi / √Var(CARi),   tBMP = √N · SCAR / sSCAR(4)

One failure can hit several competitors on the same day, and their reactions are then not independent. The BMP statistic is therefore adjusted as proposed by Kolari and Pynnönen (2010) (the BMP-KP test, Equation 5). Correlation is allowed only within a cluster c. For a pair of reactions it is the correlation of their unexplained returns in the estimation window, scaled by the share ℓij of event-window days that the two windows have in common. The p-values use a t distribution with G − 1 degrees of freedom, where G is the number of independent events; using the number of events rather than the number of reactions makes the test more cautious:

tKP = tBMP √[(1 − r̄) / (1 + (N − 1) r̄)],   r̄ = Σi≠j, c(i)=c(j) ρij / (N(N − 1)),   ρij = corr(εi, εj) · ℓij / L(5)

The equal-weighted average CAR is reported with a cluster-robust interval (Equation 6). A cluster-robust standard error measures the uncertainty of the average while allowing reactions in the same independent event to be correlated. The 95% interval is the range of values that the data cannot rule out at the 5% level. As a further check, the study also reports a wild cluster bootstrap p-value, which re-creates the test many times from the data and is more reliable when there are few clusters (Cameron et al., 2008; Webb, 2023):

SECR = (1/N) √[ G/(G − 1) · Σg (Σi∈g (CARi − CAR))2 ],   interval: CAR ± tG−1, 0.975 SECR(6)

5.4 What exactly is being averaged?

An average depends on how much weight each observation gets, and with few firms and events this choice matters. Let i = 1, …, N index competitor reactions (one per listed competitor per failure), g = 1, …, G independent events, and k = 1, …, K firms, with Ng and Nk reactions in event g and firm k, and si = √Var(CARi). Four averages answer four different questions. Statisticians call the quantity that an average is meant to measure the estimand:

θobs = (1/N) Σi CARi,   θevent = (1/G) Σg (1/Ng) Σi∈g CARi,   θprec = Σi CARi/si / Σi 1/si,   θfirm = (1/K) Σk (1/Nk) Σi∈k CARi(7)

θobs is the average reaction of a listed competitor to a failure; θevent treats every independent event equally; θprec gives more weight to calm stocks, whose reactions are measured more precisely; and θfirm treats every firm equally. The confirmatory hypothesis H2 is stated for θobs, reported with the cluster-robust interval of Equation 6. The prespecified BMP-KP test asks a slightly different question, whether the average standardized CAR is zero; the two coincide only when all stocks are about equally volatile, and the test is closest in spirit to θprec. Both are reported, each next to the quantity it matches. None of the weightings is better on statistical grounds; θobs stays the main estimate because it was chosen before the results were seen.

5.5 Event windows and prespecified checks

The main window covers day 0 and the next trading day, which is long enough to capture a failure late in the trading day and short enough to keep out most other news. Table 3 lists the windows. Fixing the windows in advance matters because choosing a window after seeing the data would make it easy to report the window with the strongest result. Nine robustness checks were fixed in advance. Check R1 averages the reactions within each independent event first and then tests the event averages, with both a t-test and a sign test, which counts how many averages are negative. Check R2 is a plain t-test that ignores standardization. Check R3 is a rank test in the spirit of Corrado (1989), with a p-value obtained by simulation. Check R4 uses the wider window. Check R5 drops or shifts events whose disclosure time is uncertain. Check R6 drops reactions estimated with the market-adjusted fallback model. Check R7 drops clusters that contain more than one failure. Check R8 leaves out one firm at a time. Check R9 is a placebo test, which repeats the whole calculation on ordinary days with no failure, to see how often an average as large as the real one appears by chance. Analyses designed after the results were seen are called exploratory, are labelled wherever they appear, and never replace H2.

6. Main results

6.1 The rocket's maker

In every one of the 4 listed cases the maker's shares fell over the two trading days after the failure became public, after allowing for the movement of the market, by between −8.8% and −16.3%, and in each case the 95% interval excludes zero. Table 4 gives the numbers; it is the main evidence on makers, and its interval column shows whether each move is larger than normal noise in that stock. Figure 5 shows the path of the abnormal return from five days before to twenty days after each failure. It matters because a two-day number cannot show whether prices were already falling before the failure, which would point to other news, or whether the drop reversed later, which would point to an overreaction. In economic terms, over the two trading days around each failure the maker's market value ended 8.8% to 16.3% below the level that the market model predicts. For a maker worth one billion dollars, this would correspond to roughly 88 to 163 million dollars in market value. Each move is between 2.3 and 6.6 times the standard deviation of an ordinary two-day abnormal return for that stock, so these are unusual moves rather than normal day-to-day noise. The size of the drops is consistent with investors expecting costs beyond the lost rocket, such as delayed launches, lost contracts and a weaker reputation for reliability, but the data do not separate these channels, and the estimates describe how prices moved around the failures rather than proving that each failure alone caused the whole move. With 4 cases from 2 firms, however, these are case studies, not an estimate of an average effect.

6.2 Listed competitors

Table 5 reports the confirmatory test. It is the central table of the paper, because it puts the main coefficient, both tests and the power figures side by side, so that the reader sees both what was found and what the sample could have found. The equal-weighted average two-day abnormal return of listed competitors is +0.28%, with a 95% interval from −1.62% to +2.18%; the cluster-robust p-value is 0.76 and the wild cluster bootstrap p-value is 0.85. The prespecified standardized test gives t = 0.85 (p = 0.40). H2 is therefore not rejected at the 5% level by any of these tests.

In economic terms, the main coefficient says that over the two trading days around a competitor's failure, the average listed competitor's shares did 0.28% better than the market model predicts. For a competitor worth one billion dollars, this corresponds to about 2.8 million dollars in market value. This is small next to the noise in a single reaction: the typical standard deviation of one competitor's two-day abnormal return is about 4.8%, roughly 17 times the average. The 95% interval runs from −1.62% to +2.18%, that is, from about 16 million dollars lower to about 22 million dollars higher for a one-billion-dollar firm, so both a small loss consistent with contagion and a small gain consistent with competition fit the data. The estimate is an association between failures and competitors' share prices; it would be read as the market's response to the failure only if no other news moved these prices in the same two days, and section 8 shows that this does not always hold.

How large would an effect have to be for this sample to find it? With a cluster-robust standard error of 0.91%, an average effect would need to be about 2.7% to be detected four times out of five (80% power), and about 3.1% to be detected nine times out of ten. The chance of detecting a true average effect of 1% is only 0.18. This figure is called the minimum detectable effect: the smallest true effect that a test of this size would usually find. A two-day move of 2.7% in a firm's value is large in economic terms, so the test can only rule out large effects. The 90% interval (−1.29% to +1.85%) could support a claim that the effect is negligible only if a two-day effect of about ±1.9% were accepted as negligible, which it is not. The data therefore do not show that competitors are unaffected; they show that any average effect is too small, relative to the noise, to be measured with this sample.

6.3 Sample concentration, repeated firm exposure, and external validity

The competitor sample is concentrated. Avio SpA appears in every one of the 22 independent events. The same shares appear in many events, so the 22 clusters are not 22 independent draws from the launch industry. Grouping by event handles competitors that react on the same day, but a firm-specific shock that lasts for months, such as a change in how investors value one firm, links that firm's reactions across events. Grouping by firm would address this, but with 3 firms such inference is not reliable.

7. Robustness and sensitivity

A robustness check repeats the analysis with a different but reasonable choice, to see whether the conclusion depends on that choice. None of the 12 prespecified checks finds a significant average at the 5% level (Figure 7 and Table 8). The figure and table matter because they show whether the main conclusion depends on one analytical choice, such as the test, the window or the treatment of uncertain dates. The conclusion that no effect is detectable is therefore stable. The size of the estimate is not: across the checks the average ranges from −0.15% (Leave out AVIO.MI) to +1.84% (Wider window, T−1 to T+3). Leaving out one firm changes the sign of the average.

8. Confounding corporate announcements and sensitivity of the competitor estimate

An event study gives the event credit for every unusual return in the window. If a firm announces something else on the same days, such as a new contract or quarterly results, its reaction mixes the two pieces of news. This problem is called confounding, and the event-study model cannot fix it by itself (MacKinlay, 1997). The news around the largest competitor reactions was therefore checked against public sources. 9. Discussion

The evidence supports two statements. First, failures are associated with large drops in the market value of the listed maker, in a small set of cases. Second, the average reaction of listed competitors cannot be told apart from zero in this sample. The first finding fits the Challenger evidence, where the market quickly singled out the firm responsible for the failure (Maloney & Mulherin, 2003). The second differs from the clear average contagion that Lang and Stulz (1992) find for bankruptcies, although their finding that competitors can gain in concentrated industries shows that the net effect depends on the industry.

Several explanations are consistent with a small net reaction of competitors, and the data cannot separate them. Contagion and competitive gains may both be present and cancel each other out, as the two channels in section 1 suggest. Customers may be unable to switch providers quickly, because rockets and satellites need long preparation and the choice of providers is limited (Dillow, 2015), which would weaken the competitive effect in the short run. Or investors may treat a failure mainly as news about the failed firm and its rocket rather than about the industry. A short-lived market-wide mood effect of the kind found after air crashes (Kaplanski & Levy, 2010) would be removed by the market model, because it moves the benchmark index as well. Separating these explanations would need customer-level data on contracts that moved between providers, launch backlogs, or a much larger sample of listed competitors.

For investors, the results suggest that, in this sample, a launch failure is mainly a risk for the firm whose rocket failed rather than for the listed industry as a whole. The evidence is too weak, however, to treat competitors' shares as unaffected, and the conclusion may change as more launch firms list and the number of independent events grows.

10. Limitations

The first limitation is the small number of listed firms. Only 3 listed firms qualify as competitors, and they produce 22 independent events, every one of which involves Avio SpA. Because the same shares appear in many events, grouping reactions by event captures only part of the dependence between them, and with 3 firms grouping by firm is not reliable either. The maker evidence is thinner still: 4 cases from 2 firms, which are case studies rather than an average effect.

The second limitation is statistical power. Average effects of about 2.7% could go undetected, so the study cannot claim that the effect is zero or negligible. The sign of the estimate depends on how events and firms are weighted, and on two large reactions that coincide with contract news. The interval (for the equal-weighted mean) and the prespecified test (for the standardized mean) answer slightly different questions and can disagree.

The third limitation is timing. 88% of competitor reactions and 4 of 4 maker cases use GCAT launch times that were not checked separately against a company statement. These times are used only where day 0 would not change if the true disclosure came up to 10 minutes earlier or 180 minutes later. One failure is dated by a statement with low confidence, which checks R5a and R5b address. A clock or time-zone error larger than that band would move day 0.

The fourth limitation is the model. A one-factor market model with a home-market index does not remove moves that affect the whole space sector on the event days (one flagged case). Firms that were listed but later delisted, such as Orbital Sciences (later Orbital ATK), Astra and Virgin Orbit, are missing from the main sample because the free price source does not carry them. Yahoo Finance prices and GCAT records are good sources but are not audited.

Finally, the results describe historically eligible, publicly traded launch firms with comparable commercial business, not the launch industry as a whole, and they say nothing about private, state-owned or diversified aerospace companies. The rules were committed to git before the results (Appendix G) but were not filed with an independent registry, and git timestamps are set by the author.

11. Conclusion

In every observed case, an orbital launch failure is associated with a drop of 8.8% to 16.3% in the listed maker's market value over two days, relative to the market. For listed competitors, the average two-day abnormal return around a failure is +0.28%, with an interval from −1.62% to +2.18%; for a firm worth one billion dollars, the interval covers anything from a loss of about 16 million dollars to a gain of about 22 million dollars. The data do not establish contagion or competitive gains, and they do not rule out either at magnitudes of a few percent.

The study is useful as a transparent and reproducible starting point. Every number can be recomputed from the published data (Appendix D), and the rules were fixed before the results (Appendix G). Adding more listed firms, including firms that have since delisted, and adding SpaceX as its price history grows, would sharpen the answer without changing the prespecified test.

Appendix B. Firm mapping and competitor criteria

Competitors are classified point in time from two hand-built files. The firm map (data/manual/firm_map.csv) records, for each firm and period, the listing dates, the ownership of each rocket, how important launch is to the firm, and the source of each fact. The size-and-market table (data/manual/vehicle_segments.csv) records the size class and customer market of each rocket. A Tier 1 competitor must be listed, have launch as a pure-play or major business, sell a rocket of the same size class to the same customer market as the failed rocket, and have flown that rocket in the previous 730 days. Tier 2 competitors are added case by case in data/manual/rival_overrides.csv, each with a source. Diversified aerospace groups are classified as having minor exposure to launch and enter only descriptive tiers, not the test.

Plain-language FAQ

What is an abnormal return?
The part of a stock's move the market cannot explain: the actual return minus what the market model predicted for that day.
Does "not detectable" mean competitors are unaffected?
No. It means the average is too small, relative to the noise in this sample, to tell apart from zero. Effects of a few percent either way remain possible.
Why are the average and the test sometimes different?
The test weights calm stocks more than volatile ones; the simple average weights every reaction equally.
Can I trade on this?
No. This is research on past reactions with a small sample, not investment advice.
