Rocket Launch Failures and Equity-Market Spillovers: Evidence from Publicly Listed Launch Providers

A T-Minus Markets case study of stock market reactions to orbital launch failures, 2000 to 2026.

Nahian Ibnat · Nov 2026 · Draft for feedback, not peer reviewed · Article · Interactive report · Code · Download PDF

Abstract

This paper asks how equity markets react when an orbital launch fails, both for the company that built the rocket and for its publicly listed competitors. Using 138 orbital launch failures from 2000 to 2026 (GCAT) and a market-model event study with a prespecified test, it finds that in the 4 failures with a listed maker (2 firms) the maker's two-day cumulative abnormal return was between −8.8% and −16.3%, each case with a 95% interval excluding zero. Across 24 failures with listed competitors (42 stock reactions, 3 firms, 22 independent events), the equal-weighted average two-day cumulative abnormal return is +0.28% (95% cluster-robust interval −1.62% to +2.18%; cluster-robust p = 0.76); the prespecified standardized test (Boehmer-Musumeci-Poulsen with a Kolari-Pynnönen adjustment) gives p = 0.40. The evidence therefore establishes neither systematic contagion nor systematic competitive gains among these firms, and it does not show the effect is negligible: average effects of about 2.7% in either direction would usually go undetected. The sign of the estimate changes with how events and firms are weighted, and every independent event involves Avio SpA. The study is a small-sample case study of listed firms, not an estimate for the launch industry as a whole.

Keywords: event study; industry spillovers; contagion; competitive effects; launch failures. JEL: G14, L13.

In short

  • The rocket's maker: large losses in each of 4 cases (−8.8% to −16.3% over two days).
  • Listed competitors: +0.28% on average (95% interval −1.62% to +2.18%, p = 0.76; prespecified standardized test p = 0.40): no detectable reaction.
  • Uncertainty: effects of about 2.7% either way could go undetected; the sign depends on weighting.

1. Introduction

When a rocket fails, the company that built it loses hardware, often a customer's satellite, and usually months of flights while the cause is investigated. Its competitors face a less obvious outcome. Investors may read the failure as a reminder that spaceflight is risky for every provider, which lowers rivals' values (contagion), or they may expect customers to move to providers whose rockets work, which raises them (competitive gain). The two channels push in opposite directions, so the net reaction of rivals is an empirical question.

This paper measures both reactions with a short-window event study. Launch failures suit the method: they are sudden, public, precisely timed, and unrelated to the rivals' own decisions. The confirmatory hypothesis (H2) is that the average two-day abnormal return of listed rivals is zero. All rules were fixed and committed to the project repository before any abnormal return was computed (Appendix G).

The answer is limited by the sample. Only 3 listed launch firms qualify as direct rivals at the time of a failure, so the study observes 22 independent rival events, every one of which involves Avio SpA. The average reaction is not distinguishable from zero, but the interval is wide enough to contain economically meaningful losses and gains. The contribution is therefore a carefully documented, reproducible estimate with honest uncertainty, rather than a general claim about the launch industry.

Section 2 describes the industry; section 3 relates the study to earlier work; sections 4 and 5 describe data and methods; sections 6 to 8 report results, robustness and confounding news; sections 9 to 11 discuss, list limitations and conclude.

2. Institutional background

A concentrated market. Orbital launch is supplied by a small number of providers, many of them state agencies, state-owned firms or privately held companies. Launch contracts are usually signed years ahead, and a satellite is designed for a particular rocket, so customers cannot switch providers quickly. This limits how much business a rival can win in the days after a failure.

Reliability. New rockets fail more often than mature ones, and a failure normally grounds the vehicle until an investigation is complete. For the maker, the loss includes the hardware, possible penalties or re-flights, delay of other launches and reputation.

Ownership and listing. Few launch companies have been publicly traded with launch as a major business. Large aerospace groups that build rockets are diversified, so a launch failure is a small part of their value; the study therefore counts them only when launch is a major part of the business. The listed pure-play or launch-heavy firms in the sample listed only in the last decade, so the sample of listed rival events starts late. SpaceX listed in June 2026 and has too little price history to enter.

Disclosure. Most launches are broadcast live, so a failure becomes public at launch time. Where a failure is confirmed later, the first public company statement dates the event.

3. Related literature

Event-study method. The design follows MacKinlay (1997) and Brown and Warner (1985) for daily returns. The standardized test of Boehmer, Musumeci and Poulsen (1991) allows the event to raise return variance, and Kolari and Pynnönen (2010) show that cross-sectional correlation among event-day abnormal returns biases standard tests, and propose the adjustment used here. Corrado (1989) supplies a rank-based alternative.

Contagion and competitive effects. Lang and Stulz (1992) find that bankruptcy announcements lower the value of a portfolio of competitors by about 1% on average, so contagion dominates in that setting. Bosch, Eckard and Singal (1998) study rival airlines after air crashes, testing whether customers switch carriers or fly less.

Disasters and investor sentiment. Kaplanski and Levy (2010) report market-wide losses after aviation disasters that reverse within days, consistent with sentiment rather than fundamentals. For spaceflight, Blose et al. (1996) and Maloney and Mulherin (2003) study the Challenger disaster: NASA contractors fell together on the day, and within days the market singled out the firm whose component failed.

Launch markets. Abramov and Kiang (2015) study how launch delays and failures affect satellite operators, the launch customers. Do et al. (2023) show that US satellite launch days draw investor attention away from firm-specific news. This paper studies a different group, the failed provider's listed competitors, over a multi-year sample.

4. Data and sample construction

Launch records. Every orbital launch attempt comes from GCAT (McDowell). A launch is a failure when its GCAT success score is below 0.75; pad explosions before launch are kept separately. Launch times come from GCAT's launch date field; where the company confirmed a failure later, the documented disclosure time is used and its confidence is recorded.

Firms. Each failure is linked by hand, point in time, to its maker if listed and to listed rivals. A Tier 1 rival is listed, has launch as a pure-play or major business, sells a rocket of the same size class to the same customer market, and has flown that rocket in the previous 730 days. Firm-map rows used in tests are verified against filings or exchange notices.

Prices. Daily adjusted closes from Yahoo Finance, used locally only; benchmarks are each firm's home market index. Prices from before a firm's real listing (a pre-merger shell) are dropped. Only derived statistics are published.

Event dates and clusters. Day 0 (T0) is the first trading session at or after the failure became public, on the stock's own exchange calendar. Failures less than 5 trading days apart that share a firm form one cluster.

Event timing verification

Day 0 depends on when a failure became public relative to the exchange close. For each event the disclosure time is either a sourced company statement (with high or low confidence) or GCAT's recorded launch time. GCAT times are used only where day 0 would not change if the true disclosure came 10 minutes earlier or up to 180 minutes later; events failing that check need a sourced time. 88% of rival reactions (37 of 42) and 4 of 4 maker cases rely on GCAT launch times that were not separately verified against a company statement; 2 rival reactions rely on a low-confidence sourced time (covered by checks R5a and R5b).

5. Empirical methodology

For each stock and failure a market model is estimated by ordinary least squares on trading days 250 to 30 before day 0 (at least 100 returns; with 30 to 99 returns a market-adjusted model with α = 0 and β = 1 is used and flagged):

Rit = αi + βi Rmt + εit(1)

Abnormal returns and cumulative abnormal returns over an event window [τ1, τ2] are

ARit = Rit − (α̂i + β̂i Rmt),   CARi(τ1, τ2) = Σt=τ1τ2 ARit(2)

Returns are simple, and CARs are sums of simple abnormal returns. The variance of each CAR includes the prediction error of the estimated model (Campbell, Lo and MacKinlay, 1997), where L is the window length, T the number of estimation days, W the event window and E the estimation window:

Var(CARi) = σ̂i2 [ L + L2/T + (Σt∈W(Rmt − R̄m))2 / Σt∈E(Rmt − R̄m)2 ](3)

The confirmatory test is the standardized test of Boehmer, Musumeci and Poulsen (1991), which uses the cross-sectional spread of standardized CARs and so allows the failure to raise variance:

SCARi = CARi / √Var(CARi),   tBMP = √N · SCAR / sSCAR(4)

Because one failure can hit several rivals, the statistic is adjusted as in Kolari and Pynnönen (2010). Correlation is non-zero only within a cluster c; for a pair it is the correlation of estimation-window residuals, scaled by the share of event-window days ℓij the two windows have in common. p-values use a t distribution with G − 1 degrees of freedom, where G is the number of independent clusters:

tKP = tBMP √[(1 − r̄) / (1 + (N − 1) r̄)],   r̄ = Σi≠j, c(i)=c(j) ρij / (N(N − 1)),   ρij = corr(εi, εj) · ℓij / L(5)
SECR = (1/N) √[ G/(G − 1) · Σg (Σi∈g (CARi − CAR))2 ],   interval: CAR ± tG−1, 0.975 SECR(6)

5.1 Estimands

Let i = 1, …, N index rival reactions (one per listed rival per failure), g = 1, …, G independent events, and k = 1, …, K firms, with Ng and Nk reactions in event g and firm k, and si = √Var(CARi). Four averages answer different questions:

θobs = (1/N) Σi CARi,   θevent = (1/G) Σg (1/Ng) Σi∈g CARi,   θprec = Σi CARi/si / Σi 1/si,   θfirm = (1/K) Σk (1/Nk) Σi∈k CARi(7)

θobs is the average reaction of a listed rival to a failure; θevent treats every failure cluster equally; θprec gives more weight to calm stocks; θfirm treats every firm equally. The confirmatory hypothesis H2 is stated for θobs (the published estimate and its cluster-robust interval, equation (6)). The prespecified test statistic is the standardized BMP-KP test, whose null is a zero mean standardized CAR; it coincides with θobs = 0 only when volatilities are similar, and is closest in spirit to θprec. Both tests are reported, each next to the estimand it matches. None of the weightings is preferred on statistical grounds; θobs stays primary because it was fixed in advance.

Table 5 reports the confirmatory test. Precision. With a cluster-robust standard error of 0.91%, an average effect would need to be about 2.7% to be detected with 80% probability; the probability of detecting a true 1% effect is 0.18. The 90% interval (−1.29% to +1.85%) would support an equivalence claim only for a margin of about ±1.9%, which is not economically negligible for a two-day return. The data therefore do not show that rivals are unaffected.

6.3 Sample concentration, repeated firm exposure, and external validity

The rival sample is concentrated. Avio SpA appears in every one of the 22 independent events. The same securities appear in many events, so the 22 clusters are not 22 independent draws from the launch industry: clustering by event handles rivals that react on the same day, but a firm-specific shock that persists across months (for example a change in how investors value one firm) links that firm's reactions across events. Clustering by firm would address this, but with 3 firms such inference is not reliable.

A firm-level view makes the limit concrete: the mean of the 3 firm means is −1.16%, with a 95% t interval of −9.1% to +6.8% on 2 degrees of freedom. The results describe these listed firms, not private, state-owned or diversified aerospace companies, and they cannot be generalized to the launch industry as a whole.

7. Robustness and sensitivity

None of the 12 prespecified checks detects a significant average at the 5% level. Non-rejection is stable; the estimate is not: across checks the average ranges from −0.15% (Leave out AVIO.MI) to +1.84% (Wider window, T−1 to T+3). Leaving one firm out changes the sign.

8. Confounding corporate announcements and sensitivity of the rival estimate

An event study attributes every unusual return in the window to the event. If a firm announces something else on the same days, its reaction mixes the two. The news around the largest rival reactions was therefore checked against public sources. Flagged reactions stay in the confirmatory test, because the rules fixed in advance do not allow removing events after results are seen.

The exploratory estimates are not more correct than the prespecified one: the choice of what to drop was made after seeing the data.

9. Discussion

The evidence supports two statements. First, listed makers lose substantial value when their rocket fails, in a small set of cases. Second, the average reaction of listed rivals cannot be distinguished from zero in this sample.

Several mechanisms are consistent with a small net rival reaction: contagion and competitive gain may both be present and offset each other; customers may be unable to switch quickly because contracts and satellites are tied to particular rockets; or investors may treat failures mainly as news about the failed firm. None of these mechanisms is identified here. Distinguishing them would require customer-level data on contract moves, launch backlogs, or a much larger sample of listed rivals. The results also say nothing about private, state-owned or diversified aerospace firms.

10. Limitations

  • Few listed firms. 3 rival firms and 22 independent events; Avio SpA appears in all of them. Repeated exposure of the same stock means clustering by event captures only part of the dependence, and with 3 firms clustering by firm is not reliable.
  • Small maker sample. 4 cases from 2 firms: case studies, not an average effect.
  • Power. Average effects of about 2.7% could go undetected; no equivalence claim is possible.
  • Weighting and outliers. The sign of the estimate depends on weighting and on two large reactions with contract news.
  • Two estimands. The interval (equal-weighted mean) and the test (standardized mean) concern different quantities and can disagree.
  • Timing. 88% of rival reactions and 4 of 4 maker cases use GCAT launch times not separately verified against a company statement (only where day 0 is insensitive to a −10 to +180 minute error); one failure is dated by a low-confidence statement (R5 checks). Time-zone or clock errors beyond that band would shift day 0.
  • Model dependence. A one-factor market model with a home index; sector-wide moves on event days are not absorbed (one flagged case).
  • Survivorship. Listed launch firms that later delisted (Orbital Sciences/Orbital ATK, Astra, Virgin Orbit) are missing because the free price source does not carry them.
  • External validity. The population is historically eligible, publicly traded launch-service competitors with comparable commercial exposure, not the launch industry.
  • Data quality. Yahoo adjusted prices and GCAT records are good but not audited sources; price-quality issues are logged in the pipeline.
  • Prespecification. The rules were committed to git before results (Appendix G) but not filed with an independent registry; git timestamps are set by the author.

11. Conclusion

When an orbital rocket fails, its listed maker loses a large share of its value within two days in every case observed. For listed competitors, the average two-day reaction is +0.28%, with an interval from −1.62% to +2.18%. The data do not establish contagion or competitive gains, and they do not rule out either at magnitudes of a few percent. The study is useful as a transparent, reproducible baseline: every number can be recomputed from the published data, and adding more listed firms, including delisted ones and SpaceX as its price history grows, would sharpen the answer without changing the prespecified test.

Appendix B. Firm mapping and rival criteria

Rivals are classified point in time from the hand-built firm map (data/manual/firm_map.csv) and the size-and-market table (data/manual/vehicle_segments.csv): listing dates, ownership, exposure and sources are recorded per row. Tier 1 requires the same size class and customer market, launch as a pure-play or major business, and a flight in the prior 730 days. Diversified aerospace groups are classified as minor exposure and enter only descriptive tiers.

Plain-language FAQ

What is an abnormal return?
The part of a stock's move the market cannot explain: the actual return minus what the market model predicted for that day.
Does "not detectable" mean competitors are unaffected?
No. It means the average is too small, relative to the noise in this sample, to tell apart from zero. Effects of a few percent either way remain possible.
Why are the average and the test sometimes different?
The test weights calm stocks more than volatile ones; the simple average weights every reaction equally.
Can I trade on this?
No. This is research on past reactions with a small sample, not investment advice.
